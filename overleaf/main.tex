\begin{abstract}
  Online judges give novice programmers immediate correction, but they were designed for contests: a failed submission returns a bare verdict such as ``Wrong Answer'', and teachers find them hard to adapt to coursework. This paper asks how an online judge can be designed to support the teaching of introductory programming, and how novices perceive it in class. Following a design-based research approach, we built WhyRunner, an educational judge that combines teacher-curated lessons, feedback that keeps hidden tests confidential, and AI hints instructed to explain the error without giving the fix. In a first classroom use, 30 first-year technical high-school students, 24 of whom had never used a judge, solved an exercise list; the mean System Usability Scale score was 72.1, above the benchmark of 68, the AI hints were the feature students valued most, and exact output matching was the main obstacle. The evidence is limited to students' perceptions in one lesson.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Automatic assessment of programming exercises is common in introductory courses \cite{alamutka2005survey,ihantola2010review}, and online judges, which run a submitted program against predefined test cases and return a verdict, are its most widespread form \cite{wasik2018survey}. For a novice, the immediacy of that correction is valuable, but its content is poor: most judges were built for contests, where fairness requires that a failed submission receive only a verdict such as ``Wrong Answer'' or ``Time Limit Exceeded''. Feedback research shows that feedback helps learning when it tells the learner where they stand and how to proceed \cite{hattie2007power}, and reviews of automated feedback for programming find that most tools report \emph{whether} a program is wrong rather than \emph{why} \cite{keuning2018systematic}. Teachers, in turn, find contest judges rigid when they want to assign an ordered list, follow each student, or require a particular approach.

Large language models (LLMs) now make explanatory feedback cheap, and tools such as CodeHelp \cite{liffiton2023codehelp} and CodeAid \cite{kazemitabaar2024codeaid} have shown that an assistant can be constrained to help without writing the solution. These assistants, however, live outside the judge: they do not see the test that failed, and a judge that simply forwards its failure report to students would reveal the hidden tests that keep grading honest. The problem addressed here is therefore how to bring guided, explanatory feedback \emph{into} the judging loop of a classroom without giving away answers or tests, and in a form a teacher can organize.

\textbf{Research question.} \emph{How can an online judge be designed to support the teaching of introductory programming, and how do novice students perceive such a judge in a real class?} We split it into two sub-questions: (RQ1) which design choices adapt a contest judge to classroom teaching, and (RQ2) how novices perceive its usability and its feedback, including AI hints, in a first classroom use.

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) A set of design principles for an educational judge, derived from the literature and an exploratory use, and their realization in WhyRunner: teacher-curated lessons with a gradebook and visual answer modes, verdicts and failure reports filtered to keep hidden tests confidential, and AI hints instructed to explain the error without giving the fix (\S\ref{sec:design}). (2) An implementation whose technical correctness was checked with a reproducible benchmark suite (\S\ref{sec:validation}). (3) A first classroom evaluation with 30 novice students, reporting usability, perceived usefulness of the feedback, and the obstacles they met, together with the changes those obstacles motivated (\S\ref{sec:evaluation-status}).

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Online judges and automated assessment.} Surveys trace automated assessment of programming back several decades and classify tools by what they check and how they report it \cite{alamutka2005survey,ihantola2010review,wasik2018survey}. Contest systems such as \textbf{DOMjudge} \cite{domjudge2024} and \textbf{BOCA} \cite{de2004boca}, the system of the Brazilian ICPC regionals, prioritize robustness and fairness: teams receive verdicts and test data stays hidden. Several platforms add a teaching layer. \textbf{beecrowd} (formerly URI Online Judge) offers a large problem bank and an Academic module in which professors create classes and exercise lists and follow students' progress \cite{bez2014uri}. \textbf{Run.codes} \cite{runcodes2024}, developed by Brazilian universities, lets teachers create lists and contests in many languages and integrates with learning environments. \textbf{CodeRunner} \cite{lobb2016coderunner} is a Moodle question type in which teachers author programming questions graded against tests inside quizzes. Academic prototypes close to ours include \textbf{Ataux} \cite{silva2021ataux}, which organizes classes solving lists through Codeforces and was assessed for usability with 71 students, and \textbf{GOJ} \cite{lima2022gamma}, which judges problems from past UnB marathons. In all of them, the feedback on a failed submission is the verdict and, at most, the test data the author chose to show.

\textbf{AI feedback in programming education.} LLMs have changed what automated feedback can be \cite{denny2024computing}: they can rewrite cryptic compiler errors into explanations novices find clearer \cite{leinonen2023error}, and assistants have been deployed in real courses with guardrails against handing out code. CodeHelp \cite{liffiton2023codehelp} answers students' questions about their code with explanations and refuses to write the solution; CodeAid \cite{kazemitabaar2024codeaid}, deployed in a large class, likewise avoids direct solutions and was assessed with students and educators. AI feedback for programming is thus not new. What WhyRunner adds is its placement inside the judge: the hint is requested on a specific failed submission, the model sees the failing test even when the student cannot, the hint mode is adapted to the answer mode (code, blocks, or Parsons), and contest organizers cap how much help is allowed. Table~\ref{tab:judge-comparison} summarizes the comparison.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

The work follows design-based research, in which an intervention for a real educational problem is designed, put into practice in an authentic setting, and refined from what that practice reveals \cite{wang2005dbr}. It comprised three phases.

\textbf{(1) Problem analysis and design principles.} The literature above, the author's experience with contest platforms, and informal conversations with students and instructors pointed to three gaps: verdict-only feedback, limited teacher control over coursework, and contest-oriented interfaces. An exploratory version was then used informally by five colleagues, with no script or questionnaire; their complaints confirmed the gaps and became changes (\S\ref{sec:implementation}). The resulting principles are presented with the design in \S\ref{sec:design}.

\textbf{(2) Development and technical validation.} WhyRunner was developed iteratively. Because feedback that is wrong or that leaks tests would undermine any teaching use, its correctness was checked with automated tests and a reproducible benchmark suite before the classroom use (\S\ref{sec:validation}).

\textbf{(3) Classroom evaluation.} \emph{Setting and participants:} a first-year class of the Técnico em Informática course integrated with high school at IFMG, on 29 October 2026; students solved one lesson with two Python problems during a regular class, with the developer present. \emph{Instruments:} the evaluation instrument is built into the system: when a contest or lesson ends, participants are invited to a questionnaire (eligibility checked on the server), with an informed-consent term, the System Usability Scale (SUS) \cite{brooke1996sus}, activity-specific Likert items, and open questions; a researcher-only page summarizes and exports the anonymous responses. \emph{Analysis:} SUS scores are computed per respondent and compared with the benchmark of 68 \cite{sauro2011sus} and the adjective scale \cite{bangor2009sus}; Likert items are summarized by their means; open answers were grouped into themes and the mentions of each theme counted \cite{braun2006thematic}; for the most frequent complaint, the stored submissions were inspected to check its cause. The study is descriptive: it has no comparison group or pre/post measure, so it addresses perception (RQ2), not learning gains.

\section{WhyRunner: Design for Teaching}\label{sec:design}

Each design principle below answers one of the gaps identified in \S\ref{sec:methodology} (RQ1).

\textbf{P1. The teacher organizes the work, not only contests.}\label{sec:class-lesson-model} Besides timed contests, WhyRunner has an asynchronous mode with three entities: a \emph{Class}, which students join with a code; a \emph{Lesson}, an ordered, teacher-curated list for one class with an optional due date; and an \emph{Exercise}, an existing problem placed in a lesson. Students hand in the whole lesson, and the teacher reviews it with free-text feedback per exercise. Each exercise scores the fraction of test cases passed, so a partially correct program earns partial credit, and a gradebook shows every student against every exercise and exports it. Contests keep marathon conventions (attempt counts, penalties, first solver) for the motivating role contests have in informatics education \cite{audrito2012contests}.

\textbf{P2. Several entry points into code.} Following the recommendation that programming education start with visual environments and move gradually to text \cite{audrito2012contests,weintrop2015block}, each exercise has an answer mode: \emph{code}; \emph{blocks}, Blockly blocks converted to Portugol, a Portuguese pseudo-language common in Brazilian courses, with the generated code shown read-only as a bridge to text; or \emph{Parsons}, shuffled lines of the teacher's solution to be ordered and indented \cite{parsons2006puzzles}. The program actually judged is always generated on the server, so grading and hints work the same in every mode.

\textbf{P3. Feedback says what failed without giving away the tests.}\label{sec:verdicts}\label{sec:test-confidentiality} A failed submission receives the verdict of its first failing case, distinguishing wrong answer, time limit, memory limit, compilation error (with the compiler output), and runtime error, and never penalizing a failure of the judge itself. Test cases are either public samples or hidden. As in BOCA \cite{de2004boca}, the failure report is filtered by viewer: on a sample the student sees the input, their own output, the expected output, and the error output; on a hidden case only the verdict, the case number, and how many cases passed. In lessons the teacher chooses which cases students see. Output may be compared exactly, ignoring whitespace, with numeric tolerance, or by a teacher-written checker for problems with several correct answers.\label{sec:output-comparison}

\textbf{P4. AI hints explain the error, not the fix.}\label{sec:ai-chain} On a failed submission the student may press ``Explain'' (Figure~\ref{fig:workspace}). The model receives the statement, the code, and the unfiltered failure report, and is instructed to explain the logical error without giving the exact fix; the report itself never reaches the student, only the model's answer. Contest organizers cap the help at one of four levels (\emph{off}, \emph{concept}, \emph{hint}, \emph{pinpoint}), revealed one level at a time, and calls are rate-limited per student. The same models help teachers author problems by drafting a reference solution, reviewing the statement for ambiguities and missing edge cases, and suggesting topic tags; drafts are only kept if the teacher saves them.

\textbf{P5. The teacher can ask for an approach, and check honesty.}\label{sec:prepublish-validation}\label{sec:similarity} A teacher may attach \emph{solution constraints} to an exercise, either structural rules (e.g., a maximum loop nesting or a forbidden construct) or a requirement in free text (e.g., ``must use Dijkstra's algorithm'') checked by the language model; a violation denies credit even when every test passes and tells the student which rule was broken. Before a problem is published, its reference solution must pass its own tests, so students never face a problem with wrong expected outputs. Finally, the owner of a class or contest can request a code-similarity report based on MOSS-style fingerprinting \cite{schleimer2003winnowing}, which is robust to renaming and reformatting; the interface states that a high score is a reason to read both programs, not proof of copying.

\subsection{Implementation}\label{sec:implementation}\label{sec:execution-environment}\label{sec:integration}

WhyRunner has two services sharing one PostgreSQL database: a Next.js web application, which holds the interface and all business logic, and a judge worker written in Rust that runs each test case in \texttt{isolate} \cite{mares2012isolate}, the sandbox of the International Olympiad in Informatics, with no network and limits on time, memory, and processes. It supports C, C++, Java, Python, Rust, and Portugol. AI calls go through a chain of models (Gemini 3.5 Flash and Flash-Lite \cite{gemini2023}, then Claude Haiku 5.5 \cite{anthropic2026haiku}), so that one provider's outage does not disable hints.

The architecture is the result of iteration. The submission queue moved from Redis to AWS SQS and finally to PostgreSQL's own notification mechanism, removing the external broker; a hosted backend and a separate API gave way to a single database and server-side actions; and execution moved from one Docker container per submission, which required root-equivalent access to the host, to \texttt{isolate}. The exploratory use by five colleagues produced three changes: each attempt shows the code that was submitted, a marathon-style leaderboard, and clearer errors when importing problems. A security review of the web layer found that some actions trusted client input and that failure reports exposed hidden test cases; both were fixed, which is the origin of the report filtering in P3.

\section{Technical Validation}\label{sec:validation}\label{sec:performance}\label{sec:perf-verdicts}\label{sec:perf-similarity}

The scripts and raw data behind these figures are in the repository's \texttt{bench/} directory. Since students act on verdicts, verdict correctness matters most: of 72 programs written to trigger a given verdict in six languages, 63 (87.5\%) received it, and accepted, wrong answer, and time limit were always right; the misses were Portugol's errors, graded as wrong answers because its runner exits normally after printing them, Python syntax errors graded as runtime errors, and one crash after a failed allocation. On realistic variations of correct output, exact comparison rejected 3 of 35 outputs with trailing spaces and 3 of 35 with Windows line endings, while whitespace-insensitive comparison accepted all, and no mode accepted a real error. The similarity detector separated 216 obfuscated copies from independent solutions with a ROC AUC of 0.998. Isolation added about 5\,ms per run, and a burst of 100 submissions, as when a whole class submits together, was graded in 51\,s by four workers on a desktop processor. Every one of 60 AI calls returned an answer, 37 of them through a fallback model, with median latencies of 13--20\,s.

\section{Classroom Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation-status}

\textbf{Participants.} Thirty students answered the questionnaire right after the lesson, all within ten minutes. Twenty-four had never used an online judge and six had used one a few times. No contest was held and no instructor answered.

\textbf{Usability.} The mean SUS score was 72.1 (median 76.2, SD 18.3, range 22.5--95; 95\% CI about 65.6--78.6), above the benchmark of 68 \cite{sauro2011sus} and in the ``good'' range of the adjective scale \cite{bangor2009sus}. The spread is wide (Figure~\ref{fig:sus-bands}): 13 respondents scored 80.3 or more, 4 between 68 and 80, 8 between 51 and 67, and 5 below 51. Integration and learnability were the strongest items (Table~\ref{tab:sus-items}); the weakest concerned autonomy, need for support (9 of 30 agreed) and confidence, consistent with a class in which 80\% had never used a judge.

\textbf{Open answers.} What helped most was the AI assistance (about 14 mentions, e.g., ``as dicas sem entregar a resposta da IA'') and the immediate correction (about 9); all seven general comments were positive, one noting that the system ``não mostra resposta, o que é muito bom, pois desenvolve o raciocínio lógico''. The most frequent complaint (about 9) was exact output matching: both problems used the \emph{exact} mode, and in one, 26 failed submissions from 13 students differed only in whitespace and would have passed under whitespace-insensitive comparison (12 of those students passed later). Many other failures came from printing the prompt of Python's \texttt{input()}. The second complaint (about 6) was that students could not see their own output or the traceback; smaller themes included a small editor, a non-scrollable AI panel, and the wish to run code before submitting. One student reported being deaf and finding the activity somewhat hard.

Switching existing problems to whitespace-insensitive comparison was deliberately left out (\S\ref{sec:limitations}), and output differing in case or wording is still rejected, since respecting the output specification is part of what a judge teaches.

\textbf{Threats to validity.} The evaluation is small and descriptive: one class, one list, one language, and a questionnaire right after a single session, with no comparison group or pre/post measure, so it shows perception, not learning gains. Thirty respondents give a reasonably stable SUS mean (about $\pm$7 points) but no subgroup analysis; novelty and social desirability (the developer was present) may have raised scores; and only students answered.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:limitations}\label{sec:future-work}

\textbf{RQ1: design.} Adapting a contest judge to teaching required changes in three places: who organizes the work (teacher-curated lessons, partial credit, gradebook), what a failure tells the student (distinct verdicts, visible samples, hidden cases kept confidential), and what help is available after a failure (AI hints instructed to explain the error, capped in contests). The classroom use suggests the first two were understood with little support, since the classroom flow was the best-rated aspect even among students who had never used a judge.

\textbf{RQ2: perception.} Novices rated usability as good, but with a wide spread and low confidence items, which indicates that a first contact with a judge is demanding for part of a class. The AI hints were the most cited help and were seen as not giving the answer, which matches what CodeHelp and CodeAid report for assistants with guardrails \cite{liffiton2023codehelp,kazemitabaar2024codeaid}; the judge's own feedback was rated lower, and the most frequent complaint, exact output matching, shows that a verdict alone did not tell students that their error was only formatting. This reinforces the motivation of the work: what novices miss is the \emph{why} of a failure \cite{keuning2018systematic}.

\textbf{Limitations.} (1) The evaluation covers one lesson and students only; contests, authoring, the gradebook, and the teacher's experience remain unevaluated, so RQ1 is answered by design rationale more than by evidence. (2) The study measured perception, not learning, and did not analyse how hints changed students' subsequent attempts. (3) Older problems still use exact comparison, the main source of frustration. (4) AI hints depend on external providers: answers vary between models and cost grows with use; and the hint model is told not to give the fix but not explicitly told to withhold the hidden test it sees. (5) Portugol errors are graded as wrong answers, and the structural constraint checker fails when a forbidden word appears only in a string or comment.

\textbf{Future work.} Repeat the evaluation with more classes, a contest, and the teacher questionnaire, ideally with a comparison group; analyse submission logs before and after hint requests to estimate the hints' effect on students' next attempts; move older problems to whitespace-insensitive comparison; review accessibility, prompted by the deaf student's report; and recognize Portugol's error messages.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper asked how an online judge can support the teaching of introductory programming. WhyRunner answers with a design in which the teacher curates lessons, feedback explains failures while keeping hidden tests confidential, and AI hints, placed inside the judging loop, explain the error without giving the fix. In a first classroom use, 30 novice students rated its usability above the usual benchmark and valued most the hints that do not give the answer and the immediate correction; their main obstacle, exact output matching, and their other complaints fed a new design iteration. The evidence is still narrow, so the next step is a broader evaluation that includes teachers and measures the effect of the hints on students' work.
